#D-Day 1944: The longest day on the Normandy coast

At dawn on June 6, 1944, the largest fleet in history entered the waters of Normandy, France. Nearly 7,000 ships, more than 11,000 aircraft and about 156,000 Allied soldiers landed or parachuted onto the German-occupied coast. Operation Overlord — whose opening day was known as D-Day — was the largest amphibious assault ever attempted, and a turning point in opening a second front in Western Europe against Nazi Germany.[^1]

## Why must it be an amphibious landing?

By 1944, the Soviet Union on the Eastern front bore most of the brunt of the land war and continuously urged the Western allies to open a second front to divide the fire. But sending an army across the English Channel to a fortified coast was a huge military problem: the Germans built the "Atlantic Wall" with concrete bunkers, minefields and obstacles.

The Allies prepared for months, with two rarely seen weapons: **logistics** and **diversionary measures**. They built floating ports (Mulberry) and ocean-going fuel pipelines; At the same time, they conducted a large-scale deception campaign (Fortitude) that made the German command believe that the main landing would take place in Pas-de-Calais, where the strait is narrowest — thus keeping the reserve force in the wrong place.[^2]

## Five beaches, one day

The attack targeted five codenamed beaches, each assigned to a country's forces:

* **Utah and Omaha** (United States). Omaha was the bloodiest place: high-walled terrain, intact defensive firepower after the bombardment missed its target, causing the first landing wave to suffer heavy casualties.
* **Gold and Sword** (UK) and **Juno** (Canada), where progress is more favorable.

Before dawn, British and American paratroopers jumped behind the lines to occupy bridges and intersections. By the end of June 6, the Allies had established bridgeheads even though they were not yet connected and had suffered heavy losses — an estimated 10,000 casualties, of which more than 4,000 were killed. But the pedal is there.[^3]

## From the bridgehead to the liberation of Europe

D-Day was just the beginning. The Battle of Normandy lasted throughout the summer of 1944, with fierce fighting in the bocage — fields divided by hedges — before the Allies broke the line and advanced to liberate Paris in late August.

The opening of the Western Front forced Germany to fight on two fronts at once, hastening the collapse of the Third Reich, leading to the surrender in May 1945. In Western memory, the image of soldiers wading through water under fire at Omaha has come to symbolize the price of regaining freedom for Europe — even though historians still recall that most German troops were decimated on the Eastern front.[^4]
