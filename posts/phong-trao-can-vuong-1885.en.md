# Can Vuong Movement 1885: The Royal Call to Arms and the Tragic Epic of Scholar-Gentry Patriots

In the early hours of July 5, 1885, following an unsuccessful preemptive attack against French colonial forces in the imperial capital of Hue, Regent Ton That Thuyet escorted the adolescent Emperor Ham Nghi out of the citadel to the mountainous sanctuary of Tan So (Quang Tri). There, on July 13, 1885, the historic *Can Vuong Edict* (Save the King) was proclaimed, igniting an impassioned nationwide armed resistance that persisted for over a decade.[^1]

## Context: An Empire Stripped of Sovereignty

Following the humiliating unequal treaties of Philastre (1874), Harmand (1883), and Patenôtre (1884), the Nguyen imperial court formally capitulated, surrendering Vietnam to the status of a French protectorate.

Nevertheless, deep ideological polarization fractured the Hue court into two factions: the peace faction advocating passive submission, and the war faction determined to resist, steered by Regents Ton That Thuyet and Nguyen Van Tuong. The war faction covertly amassed provisions, cast cannons, forged weapons, and erected fortified mountain redoubts across Quang Tri and Ha Tinh, awaiting the opportune moment to reclaim national independence.[^2]

## The Burning Citadel and the Can Vuong Proclamation

Anticipating a disarmament trap orchestrated by French General Roussel de Courcy, Ton That Thuyet launched a midnight assault on the French garrison at Mang Ca and the French Resident-General headquarters on July 5, 1885. Despite fierce valor, traditional arms proved incapable of overcoming modern European artillery. The imperial citadel fell.

Having relocated the young Emperor Ham Nghi to Tan So, Ton That Thuyet promulgated the *Can Vuong Edict* in the sovereign's name:

> *"Let those of intellect offer counsel, those of valor dedicate strength, and those of wealth contribute resources... Together, let us cleanse this profound disgrace and restore the sacred borders of our ancestors."*

The proclamation acted as a mighty gust reigniting the embers of national patriotism. Transcending social strata, Confucian scholars, rural gentry, and millions of peasantry took up arms, unified in devotion to the fatherland.[^3]

## Blazing Fortresses across the Annamite Range

The Can Vuong Movement unfolded across two vibrant phases punctuated by formidable regional uprisings:

* **Ba Dinh Uprising (1886–1887):** Led by Pham Banh and Dinh Cong Trang in Thanh Hoa, constructing an intricate fortress of mud, straw, and spiked bamboo that repelled multiple French frontal assaults.
* **Bai Say Uprising (1883–1889):** Commanded by Nguyen Thien Thuat in the reed-filled marshes of Hung Yen, perfecting elusive guerrilla warfare that bled French expeditionary units.
* **Huong Khe Uprising (1885–1896):** The movement's finest hour, orchestrated by senior scholar Phan Dinh Phung and master weapon-maker Cao Thang. Insurgents replicated modern French Gras rifles and established interconnected defensive networks across Thanh Hoa, Nghe An, Ha Tinh, and Quang Binh.[^4]

Even after Emperor Ham Nghi was betrayed and exiled to French Algeria in late 1888, the flame of Can Vuong endured until Phan Dinh Phung died of illness on Vu Quang Mountain in late 1895.

## The Sunset of the Feudal Resistance Banner

The Can Vuong Movement concluded a poignant chapter in Vietnam's anti-colonial saga. Its eventual demise conclusively demonstrated that feudal ideology and royal loyalty could no longer guide the complex task of national liberation against modern industrial imperialism. Nonetheless, the unwavering sacrifice and deep patriotism of Can Vuong provided an indispensable spiritual foundation, paving the way for the modern bourgeois and proletarian revolutionary movements of the 20th century.
