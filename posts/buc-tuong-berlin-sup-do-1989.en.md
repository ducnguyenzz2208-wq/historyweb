# The fall of the Berlin Wall: The night of November 9, 1989

On the afternoon of November 9, 1989, Günter Schabowski — spokesman for the East German Politburo — read a communiqué about new exit regulations that he had not yet read carefully. When the reporter asked when the regulations took effect, he turned over the paper, hesitated, then answered: immediately. Hours later, tens of thousands of East Berliners stood before checkpoints. The wall that 28 years of Cold War politics had built was neutralized not by a decision, but by a misunderstanding combined with crowd pressure.[^1]

## What was the wall built for?

Built on the night of August 13, 1961, the wall was not intended to prevent invasion but to prevent migration. During the period 1949–1961, about 2.7 million people left the German Democratic Republic for the West, mostly through Berlin — where the border remained open. This flow took away the very forces that the East German economy needed most: engineers, doctors, skilled workers.[^2]

The final project was about 155 km long surrounding West Berlin, including a concrete wall, a strip of open land with watchtowers, signal fences and orders to shoot at border crossers. At least 140 people were killed in the Berlin Wall area during its existence.

## Fall 1989: pressure builds

By 1989, the system was cracking from many sides at once. Mikhail Gorbachev's *perestroika* and *glasnost* policies signaled that Moscow would not use tanks to save its brother governments like in 1956 and 1968. In May, Hungary began removing its border fence with Austria, opening a way out for East German citizens on "vacation". Domestically, Monday's demonstrations in Leipzig grew from a few hundred to hundreds of thousands, with the slogan *"Wir sind das Volk"* — We are the people.

The East German leadership responded by replacing the leader: Erich Honecker was replaced by Egon Krenz in October. The new government drafted relaxed exit regulations to reduce pressure - a document that Schabowski announced at the wrong time to take effect on the evening of November 9.

Bornholmer Straße station was the first place to break up. Border guard Lieutenant Colonel Harald Jäger, not receiving clear orders and facing a growing crowd, decided to open the barrier before midnight. Other stations followed suit overnight.[^3]

## From overnight to a new European order

The event took place without a shot being fired, an outcome that was not obvious to those who remembered Tiananmen just five months earlier. In the following weeks, people carved the walls with their own hands; Pieces of concrete become souvenirs sold around the world.

The political process that followed was unusually rapid: free elections in East Germany in March 1990, monetary union in July, the Treaty of Two Plus Four signed in September with the consent of the four occupying powers, and Germany's reunification on October 3, 1990. [^4] The Soviet Union was dissolved fifteen months later.

Today only a few sections of the wall remain, including the East Side Gallery and the memorial on Bernauer Straße — where a strip of bare land in its original form shows more clearly than any photograph how thick the border was.
