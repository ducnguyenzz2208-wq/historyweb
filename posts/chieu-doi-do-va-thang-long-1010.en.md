# Edict to move the capital: Short text giving birth to Thang Long

In the fall of the year Canh Tuat 1010, King Ly Thai To issued a decree less than 250 Chinese characters long, explaining why he left Hoa Lu - the rugged capital amid the limestone mountains of Ninh Binh - to return to Dai La citadel on the Red River. It was an administrative decision, but also a declaration: the Dai Viet state was confident that it no longer needed rocky mountains as city walls.[^1]

## Why is Hoa Lu no longer enough?

Hoa Lu was chosen by Dinh Bo Linh in 968 for purely military reasons. The valley is closed, easy to defend, suitable for a government that has just finished suppressing the twelve warlords' rebellion and must always be on guard against the North. But those advantages became limitations: land was limited, waterway transportation was limited, and there was no agricultural area large enough to support a bureaucracy and a populous capital.

After four decades of existence of the Dinh and Early Le dynasties, when Ly Cong Uan ascended the throne in 1009, the needs changed. Need an operating center located in the middle of the delta, connected to towns by river, and capable of long-term expansion. The decree to move the capital clearly stated this argument: Dai La was chosen because it was in the center of heaven and earth, the terrain was wide and flat, the people did not suffer from flooding, and everything was rich and lush.[^2]

## A political argument wrapped in feng shui language

The slide opens by citing Chinese precedent - the Shang and Zhou dynasties moved the capital many times - then criticizes the Dinh and Le dynasties for following their own ideas and stationing the capital in a cramped place, causing the country's fortunes to be short-lived. That way of reasoning both borrowed the prestige of the scriptures and implicitly affirmed the legitimacy of the new dynasty.

Noteworthy is the ending sentence, when the king consulted his subjects instead of simply giving orders: a rhetorical gesture, but laying the foundation for the Ly Dynasty's more flexible style of governing compared to the previous two dynasties. History records that when the royal boat docked at Dai La, a golden dragon appeared, and the king named the new capital **Thang Long** — "flying dragon".[^3]

## A legacy that spans a thousand years

Since 1010, Thang Long continuously played the role of political and cultural center through the Ly, Tran, Early Le, Mac, and Trung Hung Le dynasties - with interruptions during the Tay Son and Nguyen dynasties, when the capital moved to Phu Xuan - Hue. On that ground, the Temple of Literature (1070), Quoc Tu Giam (1076) and the rampart system, partly excavated at the Thang Long Imperial Citadel relic site, were recognized by UNESCO as a World Cultural Heritage in 2010, on the occasion of the thousandth anniversary.[^4]

The Capital Relocation Edict is today taught in Vietnamese high school curricula as an exemplary political text — and as early evidence of the young monarchy's long-term planning thinking.
