Gutenberg's Printing Press

A type mould in Mainz drove the cost of copying knowledge towards zero, and shook European religion and science.

Around the middle of the 15th century, in the city of Mainz on the Rhine, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg combined several existing techniques—letter-casting alloys, oil-based inks, wine presses—into a new process. His workshop's most famous product, the 42-line Bible printed around 1455, only had about 180 copies. But by the end of the century, European printers had produced millions of books. The cost of copying a page of text has dropped to previously unimaginable levels.1

Where is the real invention?

Printing is not a new thing. China has been printing woodblocks since the 7th century; Tat Thang made loose terracotta letters around 1040; and the book Straight to the Heart and Essential Elements printed in metal type in Korea in 1377 — nearly eight decades earlier than Gutenberg.2

Gutenberg's contribution was a complete system for the Latin alphabet, of which the most important link was the adjustable letter mold: a device that allowed the casting of a series of lead-tin-antimony alloy letters, identical in height and square in body, at a rate of thousands per day. Without that precision, the print cannot be flat and even. He also replaced the water-based ink — which doesn't adhere to metal — with soot-based linseed oil-based ink.

Gutenberg himself did not get rich thanks to his invention. A lawsuit with creditor Johann Fust in 1455 caused him to lose most of his printing press to his partners, just as the Bible was nearing completion.

From Mainz to all over Europe

Technology spreads along with the workers. After the sack of Mainz in 1462, printers scattered across the continent; By 1500, more than 250 European cities had printing presses, and the number of books printed before that year — called incunabula books — amounted to about 28,000 editions.3

The consequences do not stop at quantity:

  • Religious Reformation. When Martin Luther published the 95 Theses in 1517, the text was reprinted and distributed throughout the German states within weeks. A theological debate within scholars became a mass movement — something impossible in the days of manuscripts.
  • Science. Tables, maps, and anatomical drawings can be accurately reproduced for the first time. The researcher in Padua and in Copenhagen correctly read the same number, and can point out each other's errors — a prerequisite for cumulative knowledge.
  • Language. Printers choose a dialect to sell the most copies, thereby contributing to the standardization of ethnic languages ​​and gradually narrowing the monopoly position of Latin.

A comparison that requires caution

Historian Elizabeth Eisenstein calls the printing press "an agent of change," emphasizing the fixity of the printed text as the foundation for modern science. Subsequent studies have argued that she underestimated the extent of early printing errors and the continuing role of manuscripts throughout the 16th century.4

That debate is still useful when we look at future information revolutions. The printing press did not by itself create the Reformation or the scientific revolution; it lowers copying costs to near zero and makes existing conflicts flare up faster, wider, and more difficult to control — a model quite familiar to the internet age.
