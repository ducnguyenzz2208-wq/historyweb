On July 4, 1776, the Continental Congress met in Philadelphia and adopted the Declaration of Independence, officially declaring the thirteen North American colonies to separate from Great Britain. The document penned by Thomas Jefferson opens with a proposition that would later resonate far beyond any of its political provisions: that all men are created equal, endowed by their Creator with certain inalienable rights, among which are life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.1

Why do the colonies want to secede?

The conflict did not start from a desire for independence but from a dispute over taxes. After the War with France and the Indians (ending in 1763), England was heavily in debt and sought to force the colonies to shoulder defense costs through a series of taxes: Stamp Duty (1765), Townshend tax, and then tea tax.

The colonists protested with the slogan "no taxes without representation" — they had no seats in the British Parliament. Tensions escalated through the "Boston Tea Party" (1773) and subsequent punitive laws. By April 1775, guns were fired at Lexington and Concord; the war actually began more than a year before independence was declared.2

One text, two layers of meaning

The Declaration has two parts that are very different in nature. The first part is a philosophical declaration on natural rights and on the right of the people to change or overthrow a government that has lost the consent of the governed - clearly influenced by the thought of John Locke and the Enlightenment movement. The latter part is much longer, listing the "sins" of King George III to justify secession.

It is that philosophy that creates the document's longevity, while also exposing its contradiction: many of the signers, including Jefferson, were slave owners. The assertion that "all men are equal" did not apply to slaves, indigenous peoples, or women at the time. This paradox later became a weapon in the hands of the excluded — from the abolitionist movement to the civil rights movement — as they demanded that America live up to its own promises.3

Echoes out to the world

The war lasted until 1783 and ended with the Peace of Paris, with the decisive assistance of France. But the Declaration's influence went far beyond the North American battlefield: it provided a linguistic template for independence and revolutionary movements around the world over the next two centuries.

One of those echoes resonated right in Vietnam: on September 2, 1945, Ho Chi Minh opened the Declaration of Independence of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam by quoting Jefferson's own sentence - a way of borrowing the words of a big country to claim the rights of a colonial people.4
