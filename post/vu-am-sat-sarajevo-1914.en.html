The Assassination at Sarajevo

A single shot at Sarajevo in June 1914 ignited the First World War within five weeks.

On the morning of June 28, 1914, in Sarajevo, capital of the Bosnian province of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, a nineteen-year-old man named Gavrilo Princip shot and killed Crown Prince Franz Ferdinand and his wife. That assassination, seemingly a single crime, after only five weeks, pulled Europe into a total war that killed tens of millions of people. No event shows more clearly how a small spark can ignite a system loaded with gunpowder.1

A Europe trapped by alliances

At the beginning of the 20th century, European powers divided into two opposing blocs: on one side the Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy), on the other side the Entente (England, France, Russia). The arms race, nationalism and struggle for colonies created a constantly tense atmosphere.

The Balkans were the hottest spot — where Austro-Hungarian ambitions collided with Serbian nationalism and Russian interests. Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia in 1908 caused deep discontent among Serbs in the region. Franz Ferdinand visited Sarajevo on a sensitive Serbian national holiday, becoming a target of the Black Hand nationalist group.2

From a crime to total war

The assassination itself almost failed: a morning bomb missed, and Princip only happened upon the royal motorcade when it took a wrong turn. But the diplomatic consequences are not accidental, but follow a chain reaction:

  • Austria-Hungary, backed by Germany, sent a harsh ultimatum to Serbia and declared war on July 28.
  • Russia mobilizes to defend Serbia; Germany responded by declaring war on Russia and then France.
  • When Germany invaded neutral Belgium to attack France, Britain declared war on Germany on August 4.

In just one week in early August 1914, the alliance system turned a regional conflict into a continental war. Historians still debate the extent of each country's responsibility, but all agree that no one really anticipated the catastrophic scale of what they were getting into.3

The legacy of "the war to end all wars"

World War I lasted until 1918, with brutal trench warfare and new killing technologies. It collapsed four empires — Germany, Austria-Hungary, Ottoman and Russia — redrawn the map of Europe and the Middle East, and set the stage for the Russian October Revolution.

More significantly, the harsh Versailles Peace Treaty imposed on defeated Germany sowed the seeds of discontent that led to the rise of fascism and World War II two decades later. Therefore, the shot in Sarajevo is often seen as the starting point of a "short century" of violence — a series of upheavals of which the Normandy Landings (D-Day) was one of the climaxes.4
