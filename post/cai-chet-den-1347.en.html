The Black Death

Twelve ships docking at Messina in 1347 began the largest demographic catastrophe in recorded history.

In October 1347, twelve Genoese merchant ships arrived at the port of Messina on the island of Sicily. Most of the sailors were dead or dying, with swollen black lymph nodes in their armpits and groin. The authorities ordered the boats to leave, but it was too late. Over the next four years, the plague swept across all of Europe and killed between one-third and one-half of the continent's population — the greatest demographic disaster recorded in written history.1

Path of pathogens

The causative agent is the bacteria Yersinia pestis, transmitted by parasitic fleas on mice and, in the pulmonary form, transmitted directly from person to person through the respiratory tract. This was only firmly established in 2011, when a team led by Kirsten Bos sequenced bacterial genomes taken from the teeth of victims buried in London's East Smithfield cemetery — ending a long-running debate over whether the Black Death was truly a plague.2

The outbreak originated from Central Asia, following trade routes to the west. A much mentioned link is the siege of Caffa on the Crimean peninsula, where Genoese merchants fled by sea and brought the disease with them. From Sicily, the epidemic spread to Genoa, Venice, and Marseille within a few months; By 1348 it reached Paris and London, in 1349 it reached Scandinavia, and in 1351 it reached Russia.

That speed reflected the achievements of the era: a dense sea and land trade network, connecting crowded cities, lacking hygiene and absolutely no concept of microorganisms.

How society reacts

Contemporary medicine attributes the cause to toxic air and planetary positions, so treatment is almost ineffective. But some administrative responses were surprisingly reasonable: Venice and Ragusa forced ships arriving from epidemic areas to wait forty days at anchor — quaranta giorni, the origin of today's word "quarantine*."

Other reactions were brutal. Rumors that Jews poisoned wells led to a series of massacres in the Rhine region, culminating in the murder of hundreds of people in Strasbourg in February 1349 — which occurred before the epidemic could reach the city. The self-flagellation movement (flagellants) spread throughout Central Europe, condemned by the Pope in 1349.3

Long-term consequences: when labor becomes scarce

The paradox of the Black Death is that those who survived in Western Europe were economically better off than before. Land and tools remained intact, while the number of workers plummeted, causing wages to skyrocket and the lords to lose their coercive power.

The authorities sought to halt that trend through legislation—the English Labor Act of 1351 fixed wages at pre-epidemic levels—but failed, and that coercion helped spark the peasant revolt of 1381. In much of Western Europe, serfdom declined over the next two centuries; In Eastern Europe, by contrast, aristocracy tightened control and serfdom endured much longer — a divergence that historical economists still use to explain the development gap between the two halves of the continent.4

The plague did not disappear after 1351. It returned in waves for more than three hundred years, until the last major epidemics in London in 1665 and Marseille in 1720.
