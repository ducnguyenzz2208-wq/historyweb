The Vietnamese Famine of 1945

Some two million died of starvation in a rice-exporting delta — modern Vietnam's greatest humanitarian catastrophe.

During the second half of 1944 to mid-1945, a terrible famine swept through the Northern Delta and midland provinces. The dead lay on the side of the road, their bodies were loaded onto carts and taken to the mass grave. The oft-cited figure — about two million people — was cited by the government of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam not long after; Although many researchers debate its accuracy, all agree that this is the largest humanitarian disaster in modern Vietnamese history.1

Why did the granary starve to death?

The paradox is that the famine occurred in a region that exported rice. The cause is a combination of many overlapping factors:

  • Wartime policies of Japan and France. After Japan entered Indochina in 1940, two governing apparatuses coexisted. The government confiscated rice according to quotas to serve the war, and at the same time forced farmers to uproot rice to plant jute and castor oil - industrial crops serving military needs.2
  • Transportation breakdown. The Allied Air Force bombarded railways and coastal sea routes, making it impossible for surplus rice in Cochinchina to be transported to the North, where there was a shortage.
  • Natural disaster. The late 1944 crop failed because of floods and cold; Stockpiles are depleted right at the time of harvest.

In other words, this was not simply a famine caused by crop failure, but a famine caused by distributive mechanisms and policies of coercion — a form of disaster that economist Amartya Sen later called a consequence of expropriated "usufruct rights" rather than an absolute lack of food.

Numbers and memories

The estimate of two million deaths is based on President Ho Chi Minh's declaration and preliminary investigations in 1945. A joint Vietnam-Japan survey in the 1990s, conducted by researchers such as Van Tao and Furuta Motoo, fieldworked many villages and recorded very high death rates in some localities, reinforcing the catastrophic scale despite giving a wide range of estimates.3

Photographer Vo An Ninh left behind a series of documentary photos of the famine — living human skeletons, mass graves — that became rare and haunting visual evidence of this event.

Background of a revolution

The famine collapsed the legitimacy of both the French colonial government and the Japanese military in the eyes of the people. The Viet Minh movement directly exploited that situation with the slogan "destroy granaries, solve famine", both saving people and gathering forces.

Just a few months after the peak of the famine, the August Revolution broke out and succeeded. Therefore, the At Dau famine was not just a tragedy in its own right: it was one of the social conditions that directly led to the political turning point of 1945, and was later mentioned as a reminder of the price of dependence.4
