Lady Trieu's Uprising, 248

Warrior Lady Trieu rode a war elephant against Eastern Wu forces with the vow to ride the storm and tread giant waves.

In 248 AD, amidst the long dark night of Northern domination under the tyrannical rule of Eastern Wu during China's Three Kingdoms era, a major armed uprising erupted in the Cuu Chan region (modern-day Thanh Hoa), led by the heroic young woman Trieu Thi Trinh alongside her brother Trieu Quoc Dat. The rebellion shook the colonial administration to its core and left an immortal declaration that echoes the indomitable spirit of Vietnamese women: "I wish to ride the tempest, tread the roaring waves, slay the giant whales of the Eastern Sea... not to bow my head and serve as a humble concubine to anyone!".1

A Long Nightmare under Eastern Wu Tyranny

Following the suppression of the Trung Sisters' rebellion in 43 AD, Northern feudal dynasties systematically intensified their administrative grip over Giao Chi and Cuu Chan. By the Three Kingdoms period, Eastern Wu under Sun Quan viewed the southern frontier as a vast reservoir of resources to sustain prolonged, costly wars against Cao Wei and Shu Han.

Imperial magistrates enforced ruthless exploitation: exorbitant levies on salt and iron, forced tribute of precious pearls, tortoiseshell, elephant tusks, and aromatic agarwood, alongside widespread conscription of young men into forced labor and foreign armies. The compounding oppression ignited deep-seated fury across all strata of the indigenous Vietnamese populace.2

Uprising from Nua Mountain to Southern Tremors

Trieu Thi Trinh was born in 225 AD in the mountainous district of Quan Trieu, Cuu Chan. Endowed with extraordinary resolve and physical prowess, she secretly gathered patriotic volunteers on the heights of Nua Mountain (Trieu Son, Thanh Hoa), training fighters day and night for the inevitable uprising.

In 248, the rebellion officially broke out. Insurgent forces swiftly stormed the district seat of Tu Pho before crossing rivers to liberate the fertile plains of Cuu Chan. When Trieu Quoc Dat succumbed to illness, the commanders and soldiers unanimously acclaimed Lady Trieu as Supreme Leader. In every engagement, clad in golden armor, wearing ivory clogs, her hair pinned with gold, and mounted atop a formidable war elephant, she struck terror into the ranks of Eastern Wu garrisons. Her momentum surged across Giao Chau, leaving provincial governors and prefects trembling in dismay.3

The Tragic Epic on Tung Mountain

Confronted with the imminent loss of the southern territory, Emperor Sun Quan dispatched general Lu Yin — nephew of the renowned strategist Lu Xun — as Military Commander and Governor of Giao Chau, commanding 8,000 elite troops backed by extensive naval fleets.

Recognizing that direct battlefield combat against the fearless insurgents was hazardous, Lu Yin employed cunning and insidious stratagems: dispensing vast hoards of gold and titles to bribe local tribal chieftains and fracture the coalition, while stooping to ignoble psychological ploys targeting the modesty of the female leader. After more than 30 ferocious battles spanning over six months, outmatched by overwhelming imperial reinforcements and logistical exhaustion, Lady Trieu withdrew to Tung Mountain (Hau Loc, Thanh Hoa) and took her own life in the autumn of 248, choosing martyrdom over dishonor.4

An Immortal Monument in the National Memory

Although ultimately crushed, Lady Trieu's 248 uprising remains a luminous milestone carrying forward the legacy of the Trung Sisters. The image of the golden-armored heroine riding a white war elephant became an enduring archetype of independence, affirming that even beneath the most suffocating colonial yoke, the self-determination of the Vietnamese people remained unquenched.
