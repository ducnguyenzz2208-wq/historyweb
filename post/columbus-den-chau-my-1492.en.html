Columbus Reaches the Americas

In 1492, a geographic error opened the first lasting contact between two halves of the world — wealth and catastrophe alike.

At dawn on October 12, 1492, after more than two months crossing the Atlantic, sailors on three ships of Cristoforo Columbus saw land - a small island in the Bahamas that he named San Salvador. Columbus believed he had reached the edge of Asia. He was wrong, and will keep that belief for the rest of his life. But that mistake opened the first permanent contact between two halves of the world, separated for thousands of years, and changed both irreversibly.1

A project rejected by many people

The idea of going west to reach the East is not new, and not as crazy as the myth "people thought the Earth was flat" - scholars at that time knew the Earth was round for a long time. The problem was size: Portuguese and Spanish experts calculated the Earth's circumference quite accurately and concluded that the sea distance to Asia was too far for ships at that time to cross. They are right.

Columbus relied on false estimates that led him to believe the distance was much shorter. He was rejected by the Portuguese court, and it took many years of persistence to persuade King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella of Spain to sponsor him, shortly after they completed their reconquest of Granada in 1492. 2 What saved him was not correct calculation, but the existence of an unexpected continent standing in the way.

"Columbus Exchange": biological and human consequences

Historian Alfred Crosby coined the name "Columbian Exchange" for the wave of exchange of organisms, goods, and pathogens between the two hemispheres after 1492 — and this is the most profound consequence:

  • Crops and livestock. Corn, potatoes, tomatoes, peppers, and cocoa spread from the Americas to the world, changing diets and populations on many continents. On the contrary, wheat, sugar cane, horses, and cows followed European ships to the New World.
  • Epidemic. This is the dire dark side. Smallpox, measles, influenza — diseases to which Native Americans had no immunity — swept through communities, causing mass deaths. It is estimated that the indigenous population has declined to levels not seen in several generations, largely due to epidemics and not directly due to war.3

Controversial legacy

For centuries, Columbus was celebrated as a symbol of discovery and progress; Many places take his name. But that perspective has changed profoundly. His policies toward the islanders of Hispaniola—forced labor, slavery, repression—are well documented in contemporary sources, including reports that led to his being captured and taken to Spain for a time.

Today, "Columbus Day" in many states and countries is changed to a day of remembrance of indigenous peoples. That change reflects a more mature reading of history: 1492 was not a simple "discovery," but the beginning of globalization — with all the wealth, violence and inequality that process brought.4
