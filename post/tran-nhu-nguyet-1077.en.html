The Battle of Nhu Nguyet, 1077

Supreme Commander Ly Thuong Kiet halted the massive Song invasion and immortalized the divine poem Nam quoc son ha.

In the spring of 1077, along the Nhu Nguyet River defense line (today's Cau River), the Dai Viet armed forces under Supreme Commander Ly Thuong Kiet decisively halted and crushed the invasion of 300,000 Song troops led by Guo Kui and Zhao Xie. The victory not only firmly secured the young independence of the Ly Dynasty, but also immortalized the divine poem "Nam quoc son ha" (Mountains and Rivers of the Southern Realm) — revered as the first Declaration of Independence in Vietnamese history.1

Preemptive Strike: A Strategic Masterstroke

In the early 1070s, Emperor Shenzong of Song and Chancellor Wang Anshi implemented comprehensive reforms to revitalize the Song Empire while actively planning an expansion southward to relieve internal domestic pressures. Detecting Song logistical buildups and mobilization at Yongzhou, Qinzhou, and Lianzhou, Supreme Commander Ly Thuong Kiet made a daring military calculation: "Waiting idly for the enemy to strike is inferior to seizing the initiative and striking first to blunt their spearhead."

In late 1075, a joint expedition commanded by Ly Thuong Kiet and Tong Dan launched a lightning cross-border strike, dismantling Song supply depots across Yongzhou before swiftly retreating to fortify defensive strongholds. This decisive blow completely disrupted the imperial court's original campaign timeline, granting Dai Viet crucial strategic initiative.2

The Fortified Ramparts of Nhu Nguyet

Returning home, Ly Thuong Kiet rapidly constructed an extensive defense network along the southern bank of the Nhu Nguyet River — a natural aquatic barrier shielding the capital of Thang Long. Earth ramparts reinforced with dense bamboo palisades stretched for nearly 100 kilometers. Behind these ramparts lay strategic military encampments and naval squadrons ready for swift counter-maneuvers.

When Guo Kui's massive legions arrived in early 1077, they were brought to a grinding halt before these impenetrable fortifications. Repeated crossing attempts on improvised rafts by Song general Miao Li were ambushed and annihilated. Furthermore, anticipated amphibious coordination collapsed when Song naval forces were intercepted and held at bay off the coast of Quang Ninh by Dai Viet admiral Ly Ke Nguyen.3

The Midnight Divine Poem and Decisive Counterattack

As Song legions grew demoralized, exhausted, and decimated by disease on the northern bank, a powerful voice suddenly resonated through the night from the riverside shrine of Truong Hong and Truong Hat:

Mountains and rivers of the Southern realm, the Southern Emperor shall dwell, Such is clearly ordained in the Celestial Book. How dare you rebellious invaders violate this land? You shall witness yourselves crushed into utter ruin.

The poem galvanized Dai Viet soldiers with fierce resolve, while sending waves of psychological dread through Song ranks, who believed the very spirits of the land had turned against them. Seizing the decisive moment, Ly Thuong Kiet launched a surprise amphibious night assault across the river, devastating the enemy encampments. Seeing the invaders shattered and on the brink of total destruction, Ly Thuong Kiet magnanimously offered an honorable negotiated peace, allowing the surviving Song troops to withdraw, thereby securing enduring diplomatic stability.4

Heritage and Historical Significance

The Battle of Nhu Nguyet stands as a pinnacle of medieval Vietnamese military art, uniting proactive defense, terrain mastery, and psychological warfare. The triumph permanently quelled the Northern empire's ambitions to annex Dai Viet, ushering in nearly two centuries of cultural flourishing and sovereignty for Thang Long.
