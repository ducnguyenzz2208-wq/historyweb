The Dien Hong Conference

The Tran court summoned the nation's elders to ask peace or war against the Mongols — and heard one answer: "Fight!"

At the end of 1284, facing the threat of the second Mongol Mongol army descending, Emperor Tran Thanh Tong invited elders throughout the country to Dien Hong Palace in Thang Long capital, held a banquet and asked only one question: should we draw peace or should we fight. The cry of "Strike!" rang out simultaneously. The Dien Hong Conference is therefore remembered as an early symbol of consensus between the court and the people in the face of invasion.1

Before the second Nguyen Mong storm

After the failure of the first invasion (1258), the Yuan Mongol empire — then master of China under Kublai Khan — prepared a much larger scale attack. Using the excuse of "borrowing a way to attack Champa", in 1285 the army commanded by Thoat Hoan approached Dai Viet's border.

The Tran Dynasty clearly understood the relationship between unfavorable forces. In that context, mobilizing the entire population — not just the imperial army — became a condition of survival. The Dien Hong Conference was part of that effort: a political gesture to unite the will of all people in the determination to resist.2

Two conferences, two levels of consensus

History books often mention Dien Hong in parallel with another conference that took place earlier - the conference of princes and generals in Binh Than (1282), where military plans were discussed and generals were appointed to lead the army. If Binh Than is where the ruling class agrees on strategy, then Dien Hong is the place to find consensus from the elders - those who represent the village and have prestige with the people.

It was during this period that symbols of the determined fighting spirit were born: soldiers liked to have the words "Sat That" (kill the enemy That Dat) on their arms, and Tran Quoc Tuan wrote Hich General Si to encourage the three troops. The Dien Hong Conference was part of that spiritual preparation, creating what historians call the "Aura of Dong A".3

A timeless icon

Historically, records about Dien Hong are quite brief, and researchers caution against imagining it as a modern parliamentary mechanism — it was still a monarchy, and "consultation" was more ceremonial and motivational than a decisive vote.

But precisely because of its symbolic weight, the image of the Dien Hong conference is constantly cited by later generations whenever it is necessary to talk about national unity. With two resounding victories over the Yuan army, including the Bach Dang Victory in 1288, Dien Hong became a part of the memory of the Tran Dynasty - a period when military strength was built on the foundation of "the king and subject are united, brothers are in harmony, and the whole country contributes".4
