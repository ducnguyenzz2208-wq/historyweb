The Atomic Bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki

In August 1945, two atomic bombs levelled two Japanese cities and ushered humanity into the nuclear age.

At 8:15 a.m. on August 6, 1945, an atomic bomb exploded over Hiroshima. Three days later, the second bomb fell on Nagasaki. In an instant, each city was largely flattened, and the total death toll—both immediate and subsequent from burns, trauma, and radiation—came to about two hundred thousand, most of them civilians. This was the first and only time nuclear weapons were used in war, and it opened an era in which humanity was capable of self-destruction for the first time.1

The road to the bomb

This weapon was a product of the Manhattan Project — a huge secret scientific-military effort of the United States, launched in part due to fears that Nazi Germany was also researching atomic bombs. Thousands of scientists, many of them refugees from Europe, worked under the leadership of J. Robert Oppenheimer. The first test, codenamed Trinity, was successful in the New Mexico desert in July 1945 — after Germany had surrendered.2

By the summer of 1945, the war in Europe was over, but Japan had not yet surrendered. The US command faced a choice between a landing on the Japanese main island - expected to cause huge casualties on both sides - and using new weapons to force Japan to quickly surrender.

Decisions and endless debates

Few historical decisions have been as widely dissected as the dropping of the atomic bomb. The main arguments, existing in parallel until now, include:

  • Military justification: two bombs helped Japan surrender without a bloody invasion, thereby "saving" the lives of American soldiers and Japanese civilians who would have died in a long war.
  • Ethical and historical criticism: some historians have argued that Japan was near exhaustion and could have surrendered without bombs, especially after the Soviet Union declared war on August 8; that targeting populated cities is an attack on civilians; and that an ulterior motive was to pressure the Soviet Union in the coming world order.3

Japan announced its surrender on August 15, officially signed the document on September 2, 1945, ending World War II.

The nuclear shadow of the post-war world

Hiroshima and Nagasaki did not just end a war; they shape the next era. The horror of the destructive power of atomic weapons became the psychological foundation of the Cold War, as the United States and the Soviet Union engaged in a nuclear race under the shadow of the possibility of "mutual destruction".

At the same time, the memory of the two cities became the driving force for the nuclear disarmament movement and subsequent control treaties. Every year, the memorial service at Hiroshima Peace Park reiterates a message that has transcended Japan: that this weapon should never be used again.4
