The Xinhai Revolution

The 1911 Wuchang uprising toppled the Qing, ending two millennia of monarchy and founding the Republic of China.

On the night of October 10, 1911, uprising soldiers in Wuchang opened fire against the Qing government. That seemingly local mutiny quickly spread throughout the provinces, and in just a few months overthrew the Manchu Qing dynasty — and ended a monarchy that had existed for more than two thousand years in China. In early 1912, the Republic of China was born, the first republic in mainland Asia.1

A corrupt dynasty

By the end of the 19th century, the Qing Dynasty was severely weakened by a series of defeats: the Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion, the defeat by Japan in 1895, and intervention by other powers after the Boxer Rebellion. China was divided into zones of foreign influence, and the court was powerless to reform.

In that context, revolutionary ideas spread, especially among intellectuals and international students. Sun Yat-Sen (Sun Dat-sen) gathered revolutionary groups into Dong Minh Hoi, stating the famous platform "Three Peoples": nation (overthrow the Manchus, gain national independence), civil rights (establish a republican government) and people's livelihood (improve people's lives).2

From Wuchang spread throughout the country

The Wuchang Uprising broke out somewhat by accident — a leaked bomb explosion forced revolutionary officers to act early. But it sparked a chain reaction: province after province declared secession from the Qing Dynasty.

The main developments happened very quickly:

  • The southern and central provinces became independent and established local military governments.
  • Sun Yat-sen returned from abroad and was appointed provisional Grand President of the Republic of China in Nanjing, sworn in on January 1, 1912.
  • To avoid a prolonged civil war, the revolutionaries made a compromise with Yuan Shikai — the Qing Dynasty's most powerful military general: Yuan would force the emperor to abdicate, in exchange for the presidency.

On February 12, 1912, the last emperor Puyi, then only six years old, officially abdicated. Two thousand years of imperial rule ended.3

An unfinished revolution

Xinhai succeeded in abolishing the monarchy, but failed to build a strong republic. Yuan Shikai quickly seized power, even attempting to declare himself emperor; After his death in 1916, China fell into a long period of military chaos. Sun Yat-sen's civil rights and civil rights goals remained largely unfinished.

However, the significance of 1911 extends beyond China's borders. It strongly encouraged nationalist movements throughout Asia, including Vietnam - where patriots like Phan Boi Chau closely followed and were directly influenced by Chinese revolutionary ideology. Tan Hoi is therefore considered one of the opening milestones for the wave of national liberation in the East in the 20th century.4
