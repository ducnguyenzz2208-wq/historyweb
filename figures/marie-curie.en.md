Marie Curie (1867–1934) was the first scientist to win two Nobel Prizes in two different fields, a pioneer in the study of radioactivity, and one of the figures who shaped modern physics and chemistry — in an era when science was largely closed to women.

## From Warsaw to Paris

Born in Warsaw under Imperial Russian rule as Maria Skłodowska, she was not allowed to study at university in her home country because she was a woman. She worked as a tutor while saving money to go to Paris, study at Sorbonne University in poverty, and rise to the top of her class. Here she met and married physicist Pierre Curie, who became her research partner.[^1]

## Discovery of radioactivity

Based on Henri Becquerel's discovery of strange rays emitted from uranium, the Curie couple rushed to research the phenomenon that Marie herself named "radioactivity". By processing tons of pitchblende ore under primitive conditions, they discovered two new elements: polonium (named after her native Poland) and radium. In 1903, Marie and Pierre and Becquerel received the Nobel Prize in Physics.[^2]

## Two Nobel Prizes and their price

After Pierre died in an accident in 1906, Marie continued her work and became the first female professor at the Sorbonne. In 1911, she received her second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for isolating pure radium. During World War I, she developed mobile X-ray vehicles to treat wounded soldiers on the front. Years of exposure to radioactive substances without fully understanding the toxicity had ruined her health; Marie Curie died in 1934 of a blood disease, almost certainly related to radiation exposure.[^3]

## Analysis

Marie Curie's legacy is two-layered. In science, her work paved the way for nuclear physics, cancer radiation therapy and the atomic age. In society, she is a symbol of breaking down gender barriers in science — but her own life also shows the price of that: she had to be much better to be recognized as an equal, and had her personal life attacked by the press in a way that was rarely aimed at male colleagues. Her principle of not patenting the radium separation process, so that science can develop freely, also speaks to the concept of knowledge as a common property of humanity.
