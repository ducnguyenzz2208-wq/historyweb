From a tycoon of Lam Son land, Le Loi (1385–1433) became the leader of a ten-year resistance war to expel the Ming army, regain independence for Dai Viet and found the Le Dynasty - the longest dynasty in Vietnamese feudal history.

## The person who raised the flag in Lam Son

In the early 15th century, after the Ho Dynasty failed and the Ming Dynasty imposed harsh colonial rule, many uprisings broke out and were quelled. In 1418, Le Loi raised the flag of rebellion in Lam Son (Thanh Hoa). The first years were extremely difficult: the force was small, many times they were trapped on Chi Linh mountain, sometimes having to kill elephants and horses to keep their breath. It was during this period that famous gods such as Nguyen Trai, Le Lai, and Nguyen Chich gathered under his flag.[^1]

## The policy of "fighting the weak against the strong"

The turning point came when the insurgents followed Nguyen Chich's plan, turned to Nghe An as a stepping stone, and from there advanced north. Le Loi knew how to combine military power with Nguyen Trai's strategy of "hitting people's hearts" - luring surrender, being lenient, maintaining peace and filial piety. After the victories of Tot Dong - Chuc Dong and Chi Lang - Xuong Giang, he accepted the Ming army to withdraw to the country through the Dong Quan Oath at the end of 1427, instead of destroying it to the end.[^2]

## The first king of the Le Dynasty

In 1428, Le Loi ascended the throne, known as Le Thai To, named the country Dai Viet, and established the capital in Dong Kinh (Thang Long). He promulgated a military policy to redistribute land, restore academic elections and reorganize the state apparatus after the war. The reign he inaugurated lasted — including the Renaissance of Le — for nearly four centuries.[^3]

## Analysis

The special thing about Le Loi is his successful role transition from an insurgent leader to an institution builder. Many insurrection leaders were good at leading troops but failed when in power; Le Loi, with the support of the Confucian intellectual class, laid the foundation for a regulated, centralized state. The way he ended the war — paving the way for the enemy to retreat with honor — also showed long-term strategic thinking: maintaining independence but not making a mortal enemy with a large neighboring country. That was a diplomatic lesson that was followed by subsequent dynasties. His career is closely associated with the Lam Son Uprising and with the strategist Nguyen Trai.
