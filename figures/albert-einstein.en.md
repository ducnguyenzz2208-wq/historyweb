Albert Einstein (1879–1955) was a theoretical physicist who radically changed the way people understand space, time and the universe. His theory of relativity, along with his contributions to quantum physics, made him an icon of scientific genius in the 20th century.

## The magical year 1905

Born in Germany to a Jewish family, Einstein was an unremarkable student and started his career as an examiner at a patent office in Switzerland. It was during that time, in 1905 — known as *annus mirabilis* (the year of miracles) — that he published four papers that shook physics: on the photoelectric effect, Brownian motion, special relativity, and the equivalence of mass and energy, condensed in the equation E=mc².[^1]

## General relativity

In 1915, Einstein perfected the general theory of relativity, describing gravity not as a force in the Newtonian sense, but as a curvature of space-time caused by mass. His prediction that light bends as it passes near the Sun was confirmed by observing the 1919 solar eclipse, making him a global celebrity almost overnight. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1921, primarily for his work on the photoelectric effect.[^2]

## Scientists and common sense

A Jew, Einstein left Germany when the Nazi regime came to power and settled in the United States. In 1939, he signed a letter to President Roosevelt warning of the possibility of Germany building an atomic bomb — an action he later expressed regret upon seeing the consequences. In the last years of his life, he spoke out strongly for peace, nuclear disarmament and human rights.[^3]

## Analysis

Einstein's legacy has two intertwined aspects. As for science, his theories are more than just abstractions: they underpin everyday technologies like the GPS navigation system (which must be calibrated according to the theory of relativity), and paved the way for modern cosmology and nuclear physics. Symbolically, Einstein shaped the image of the "scientist" in popular culture to such an extent that his name became a synonym for genius. But perhaps more memorable is that he represents the model of a socially responsible scientist — one who understands that knowledge is inseparable from its ethical consequences, a lesson that is especially poignant when juxtaposed with the Atomic Bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.
