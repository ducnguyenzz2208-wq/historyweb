Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), known as the Mahatma ("great soul"), was the leader of India's nonviolent independence movement — a method of political struggle that inspired civil rights and liberation movements around the world.

From a lawyer in South Africa

Gandhi was born in Gujarat, went to London to study law and then worked in South Africa, where he directly experienced racism against people of Indian origin. It was here, for more than twenty years, that he developed and tested the method he called satyagraha — "holding the truth," that is, persistent struggle through nonviolent disobedience in the face of unjust laws. When he returned to India in 1915, he was already a seasoned activist.1

Non-violence against imperialism

Gandhi transformed the struggle for independence from an elite activity into a mass mass movement. Typical campaigns include the non-cooperation movement, boycotting British goods and self-weaving (symbol of a hand-cranked cart), and most notably the Salt March in 1930 - walking more than 300 km to the sea to make salt to protest the colonial salt monopoly. He was imprisoned many times, and used hunger strikes as a moral weapon.2

Independence and the tragedy of separation

India became independent in 1947, but not in the way Gandhi wanted: the subcontinent was divided into India and Pakistan along religious lines, leading to violence and migration that left hundreds of thousands dead. Gandhi opposed partition and devoted his efforts to healing the Hindu-Muslim conflict. In early 1948, he was assassinated by a radical Hindu nationalist who thought he was too lenient towards Muslims.3

Analysis

Gandhi left behind a legacy that is both great and debated. His nonviolent methods demonstrated that a disciplined mass movement could shake an empire, and it directly influenced Martin Luther King in America or Nelson Mandela in South Africa. But modern researchers also see him as more complex than his saintly image: his youthful views on race in South Africa, or his approach to the caste system, are controversial points. It is that complexity that makes him a real historical figure, rather than a flat icon — and the central question he raises, whether the means of struggle are suitable to the ends, remains topical.
