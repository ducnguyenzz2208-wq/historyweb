Alan Mathison Turing (1912 – 1954) was an English mathematician, logician, cryptographer and theoretical biologist. Hailed as the father of theoretical computer science and artificial intelligence, Turing played a vital role in the Allied victory in World War II when he successfully cracked Nazi Germany's Enigma code machine, saving millions of lives and shortening the war in Europe by at least two years.

1. Mathematical genius and the concept of "Turing Machine"

Alan Turing was born on June 23, 1912 in Maida Vale, London. Right from his time at Sherborne School, he showed an unusual passion for mathematics and experimental science, being able to solve complex calculus problems on his own even before being taught by teachers.

In 1931, he entered King's College, Cambridge University. In 1936, at the age of 24, Turing published a landmark scientific paper titled "On Computable Numbers, with Application to the Entscheidungsproblem".

In this article, he introduces the theoretical concept of a Turing Machine — an abstract device that operates on ribbons of symbols according to predetermined rules. The Universal Turing Machine is capable of simulating any calculation, becoming the fundamental mathematical model for all modern electronic computers today.

2. Bletchley Park hero: Cracking the Enigma code

When World War II broke out in 1939, Turing joined the British Government's Cryptographic School at Bletchley Park — the Allied top-secret code-breaking intelligence center.

The biggest challenge at that time was the Enigma cryptographic machine used by the Nazi army. With millions and billions of configuration combinations changing every day, the German military absolutely believed that the Enigma code was "unbreakable". In particular, the German U-boat fleet was choking British supply shipping routes across the Atlantic.

Turing pioneered the design of an electromechanical machine called the Bombe. The machine used lightning-fast logic elimination, testing thousands of possible configurations at once to find the Enigma decryption key in just a few hours. The work of Turing and his colleagues at Bletchley Park (operation "Ultra") helped the Allies read the top secret military orders of the German Naval Fleet, playing a key role in the Battle of the Atlantic and the Normandy landings (D-Day). Historians estimate that cracking the Enigma code shortened the war by 2 to 4 years and saved more than 14 million lives.

3. Birth of Artificial Intelligence and the Turing Test

After the war, Turing worked at the National Physical Laboratory (NPL), where he designed the Automated Computing Engine (ACE) — one of the world's first stored-program computer designs.

In 1950, in his classic paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", Turing paved the way for artificial intelligence (AI) when he posed the famous question: "Can machines think?".

To answer this question, he proposed the Turing Test (Turing Test): if a human converses via text with a computer that cannot distinguish whether the communication object is human or machine, then that machine is considered to possess artificial intelligence. To date, the Turing test is still a classic philosophical and technical measure in AI research.

4. Injustice tragedy and late glory

Despite his great achievements, the last years of Alan Turing's life were mired in tragedy and injustice. In 1952, he was criminally convicted for his homosexual orientation (which was considered a crime by British law at that time) and was forced to undergo "chemical castration" therapy with estrogen hormone injections, depriving him of the right to access national security research.

On June 7, 1954, Alan Turing died at the age of 41 from cyanide poisoning, next to a bitten apple - a tragic death that shocked the conscience of the era.

It was not until 2009 that British Prime Minister Gordon Brown officially apologized publicly for his cruel treatment, and in 2013, Queen Elizabeth II granted a Royal Pardon to Alan Turing. Today, the most prestigious award in the world of computer science — considered the "Nobel Prize for informatics" — is called the Turing Award (Turing Award), and his image is solemnly printed on the 50 pound banknote, honoring a great man who has completely changed human history and technology.
