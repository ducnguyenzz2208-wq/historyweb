Nguyen Hue (1753–1792), also known as Emperor Quang Trung, was one of the most outstanding military leaders in Vietnamese history — he never lost a major battle, defeated both the Siamese army in the south and the Qing army in the north, and initiated many bold reforms during his short life as king.

From Tay Son to the throne

Nguyen Hue was the middle brother of three brothers who led the Tay Son movement that started in Binh Dinh in 1771. The movement successively overthrew Lord Nguyen in Dang Trong and then Lord Trinh in Dang Ngoai, ending the country's division that lasted two centuries. His military talent was revealed early in the battle of Rach Gam - Xoai Mut in 1785, destroying the Siamese army that Nguyen Anh requested for help.1

Rach Gam – Xoai Mut and Ngoc Hoi – Dong Da

His two greatest victories were both battles that quickly destroyed the invaders. At the end of 1788, the Qing Dynasty sent troops to use the pretext of propitiating Le. Nguyen Hue ascended the throne, took the reign name Quang Trung, then marched quickly to the north and greatly destroyed the Qing army during the five days of the Year of the Rooster in 1789. After the victory, he cleverly used flexible diplomacy to keep the peace on the northern border.2

Unfinished reformer

During his short time on the throne, Quang Trung showed the vision of a reformer: encouraging agriculture through agricultural extension decrees, promoting Nom as official writing, establishing the Sung Chinh Institute to translate books, and reorganizing the military and finances. He died suddenly in 1792 at the age of 39, many of his plans were unfinished; The Tay Son Dynasty weakened quickly and collapsed in 1802.3

Analysis

Quang Trung is often remembered first and foremost as a military genius with a talent for moving quickly, keeping secrets, and negotiating many deals. But what makes historians regret is the reform aspect: his policies promoting Nom and real learning were ahead of their time, and if given more time, could have taken the country to a different trajectory. His early death turned him into one of Vietnam's biggest "historical assumptions" — the question "if Quang Trung had lived twenty more years". His typical victory is told in the Ngoc Hoi - Dong Da Victory.
